ABSTRACT
Accurate navigation in outdoor environments requires integrating multiple
sensor sources to improve localization and trajectory tracking performance. This study
proposes a modified version of the Pure Pursuit (PP) algorithm, called Pure Pursuit with
Dynamic Steering Control (PP-DSC), which adaptively adjusts both the lookahead
distance and velocity based on the steering angle. The algorithm was deployed on a
four-wheeled steering-type autonomous mobile robot (AMR) using the Robot
Operating System 2 (ROS 2) Jazzy framework. Real-time data were fused from the
Global Navigation Satellite System with Real-Time Kinematic corrections (GNSSRTK), an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), and wheel encoders to enhance
localization accuracy. Experiments were conducted on straight, circular, and figureeight trajectories at speeds ranging from 1.0 to 5.0 meters per second. Results showed
that PP-DSC reduced the mean lateral deviation to 0.05 meters on straight paths and
below 0.08 meters on curved paths, whereas the standard PP algorithm exhibited
maximum deviations up to 0.44 meters. The findings confirm that PP-DSC improves
trajectory tracking accuracy and motion stability, particularly during directional
transitions, making it well-suited for real-time autonomous navigation under varied
terrain and motion dynamics.

INTRODUCTION

1.1

Introduction and background
A robot is a programmable machine capable of performing various tasks

through predetermined movements, and its operations are pre-planned to execute
specific functions. In the context of agricultural mobile robots, the ability to
autonomously navigate in diverse environments is crucial. Navigation involves
directing the robot's movement from one point to another, employing various sensors
suitable for different environments such as indoor and outdoor settings. Various
navigation techniques, including artificial intelligence, are employed. Robot navigation
refers to a mobile robot's capability to determine its own location, map a path to a target
location, and efficiently plan routes. Effective path planning relies on a combination of
localization and resolving the robot's current state and the target location within the
same coordinates. Mapping involves notations describing the robot's position with
reference to its surroundings. The movement of the robot from one point to another is
facilitated by navigation, where the robot autonomously decides its current position and
maps a path to the target location. The efficiency of path planning depends on the sensor
systems applied to the robot, considering different environmental conditions. For
instance, utilizing Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) in open spaces may not be as
suitable as Global Positioning System (GPS) and Real-time kinematic positioning
(RTK) due to the vast and unobstructed nature of the environment.
The field of robotics in agriculture is a widely researched area nowadays due to
its potential in developing automated systems suitable for agricultural tasks and
addressing challenges in farming practices. This includes issues like labor shortages,
increased labor costs, and the need for precision in agricultural operations. The
objective of research in this field is to develop autonomous machinery systems that can
work independently without human control, achieving higher precision and efficiency
[1–4]. Various operational aspects need attention, covering elements like localization,
navigation, sensing, actuation, and communication.

2

The operation of the robot requires the use of various sensors, including satellite
positioning systems, laser sensors, and inertial measurement units (IMU). The
utilization of LiDAR sensors is highly popular in the navigation of robotic systems due
to their accuracy and diverse measurement range. However, there are still many
limitations in the use of LiDAR sensors, such as the distance between objects and the
sensor [5,6].
In the contemporary context, there might be instances of employing the Pixhawk
control board for certain tasks, but it is perceived to lack flexibility in development.
Therefore, it is envisaged to utilize The Robot Operating System (ROS2), which is a
versatile and open-source platform crafted to assist in the development of robotic
applications [7]. ROS encompasses an extensive array of functionalities, such as
hardware abstraction, device drivers, libraries, visualizers, message-passing, and
package management. This system parallels other robot frameworks like Player,
YARP, Orocos, CARMEN, Orca, MOOS, and Microsoft Robotics Studio in terms of
capabilities.
The primary aim of ROS2 is to promote the reuse of code in the field of robotics
research and development. This objective has led to its swift adoption by a multitude
of research institutions and companies as their preferred framework for development.
ROS2 finds its application across a diverse spectrum of robotic domains, including
aerial

and ground

robots,

humanoids,

and underwater

vehicles.

Notable

implementations involve humanoid robots such as PR-2 and REEM-C, ground robots
like the Clear Path platforms, and various aerial platforms developed by Ascending
Technologies.
Moreover, ROS2 provides substantial support for a wide range of popular
robotic sensors, including Inertial Measurement Units, GPS receivers, cameras, and
lasers. These sensors are all readily accessible through extensive driver support. Despite
its foundation in Linux and the absence of guarantees for hard real-time system
properties, ROS2 is proficient in managing soft real-time applications and seamlessly
integrates with other prominent open-source libraries, including OpenCV, Point Cloud
Library, and the Gazebo simulator.
The runtime architecture of ROS2 is characterized by a peer-to-peer network of
processes, which may be distributed across multiple machines. These processes

3

communicate using a variety of methods, including asynchronous, synchronous, and
data storage communications, all underpinned by ROS2 robust communication
infrastructure.
In contemporary agricultural applications, robots have become increasingly
employed to perform repetitive and hazardous tasks across a diverse range of indoor
and outdoor environments [8,9]. Despite their growing adoption, achieving reliable
autonomous navigation remains a substantial challenge, particularly in complex
settings such as greenhouses where Global Positioning System (GPS) signals may be
unavailable [10]. To successfully traverse from a designated origin to a defined
destination, agricultural robots must integrate several essential capabilities, including
robust localization, environmental mapping, effective path planning, and obstacle
avoidance [11]. Among these, the ability to accurately establish their position and
generate a globally consistent real-time map of the surroundings is paramount for
enhancing overall performance. Consequently, there is a critical need for an efficient
and precise real-time localization and mapping method to meet the stringent demands
of autonomous navigation in agricultural environments [12].
In response to these limitations, the research community has increasingly turned
to sensor fusion strategies to enhance robust localization. By integrating Global
Navigation Satellite System Real-Time Kinematic (GNSS-RTK) data, which offers
centimeter-level precision under ideal conditions, with additional sensor modalities,
researchers have achieved more reliable and accurate navigation [13]. Inertial
Measurement Units (IMUs) provide continuous orientation, angular velocity, and
acceleration data, thereby mitigating reliance on intermittent external references. Visual
odometry, leveraging camera-derived feature tracking, further augments positioning
estimates in environments where GNSS signals may be weak or unstable. Beyond these
modalities, Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) sensing introduces rich threedimensional spatial information, ensuring continuous localization performance even
during temporary GNSS outages [14,15].
Recent studies have proposed a variety of approaches to improve localization
and tracking accuracy in autonomous systems. These include the integration of GNSSRTK with MEMS-IMU for better tilt compensation[16]. , improvements in planting

4

precision through sensor fusion[17]., and the application of Pure Pursuit with both fixed
and dynamic lookahead adjustments[18-19]. Some methods incorporate fuzzy control
[20] or advanced model-based controllers[21] to reduce lateral deviation, particularly
in curved or variable environments. Across these works, dynamic lookahead techniques
that adjust based on vehicle speed or path curvature have shown significant error
reduction, making them especially effective for autonomous agricultural vehicles and
unmanned ground systems operating in real-world scenarios.
Furthermore, research has demonstrated that combining GNSS-RTK,
microelectromechanical systems (MEMS)-based IMUs, and stereo visual odometry
significantly enhances localization robustness in complex agricultural environments
[22]. Similarly, using IMUs, differential GPS, and visual odometry within an Extended
Kalman Filter (EKF) framework has shown reliable performance where standalone
GNSS signals are insufficient [23,24]. These techniques are particularly critical in
regions like Japan, where aging agricultural workforces, labor shortages, and increased
accident risks demand autonomous solutions with robust localization capabilities. The
relevance of these sensor integration strategies also extends beyond agriculture.
Autonomous ground vehicles, unmanned aerial systems, and service robots operating
in industrial or urban environments also benefit from sensor redundancy and data
fusion. When paired with advanced path planning algorithms (e.g., A-Star) and
trajectory-tracking controllers (e.g., Pure Pursuit), these systems deliver improved
positional accuracy and robustness in dynamic environments [25]. These approaches
help mitigate localization drift and ensure safer and more reliable autonomy in realworld applications [26].
This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides a detailed discussion of
the system design, including hardware and software components, data communication
protocols, and the implementation of navigation algorithms, with a focus on improving
the Pure Pursuit algorithm for higher-speed operation. Section 3 presents the
experimental results conducted in three types of paths: line path, loop path, and figureeight path. The evaluation is based on data collected from GNSS-RTK, IMU, and
encoder sensors to assess tracking accuracy. Section 4 concludes by comparing the
performance of the proposed system with the traditional Pure Pursuit algorithm.

2.1.1 Exploiting the Internet Resources for Autonomous Robots in Agriculture
The incorporation of autonomous robots into agricultural practices

represents a pivotal development in the evolution of modern farming. In their 2018
paper, Exploiting the Internet Resources for Autonomous Robots in Agriculture,
examine this progression, emphasizing the role of Internet technologies such as the
Internet of Things (IoT) and cloud computing in augmenting the functionality of these
robots [27]. The paper highlights various applications, including the development of a
cost-effective, safe, and environmentally friendly pesticide spraying system and the
deployment of autonomous orchard vehicles in the USA for tasks like mowing, pruning,
training, spraying, blossoming, fruit thinning, harvesting, and sensing [28].In Europe,
the RHEA fleet (see Figure 1) ,comprising three tractors that collaborate in pesticide
application, is another notable example of this technological advancement.
Additionally, specialized robotic systems like LadyBird in Australia and Vibro Crop
Robotti in Europe demonstrate further innovations [29]. LadyBird, equipped with
thermal and infrared detection systems, hyperspectral cameras, stereovision cameras,
LIDAR, and GPS, is designed for crop evaluation. The Vibro Crop Robotti emphasizes
meticulous seed planting and the mechanical purification of row crops. It utilizes
computing systems that vary from centralized to basic distributed designs, facilitating
the control of a small selection of sensors and the operation of fundamental farming
implements.

The integration of advanced Internet technologies is revolutionizing
agricultural robotics. This integration involves using cloud services for data storage (see

The architecture proposed in the paper is based on the ROS2 and

The study's analysis of transfer speed, latency, and processing time

bring significant advancements in agricultural practices.
The integration of Internet resources in agricultural robots signifies a leap
forward in precision agriculture. It enhances the efficiency, accuracy, and sustainability
of farming practices, paving the way for a more technologically advanced agricultural
future.
2.1.2 Low-Cost Robot for Agricultural Image Data Acquisition
The paper "Low-Cost Robot for Agricultural Image Data Acquisition"
[32] presents a comprehensive study on the development and application of a low-cost
autonomous robot, named DARob, for image data acquisition in agriculture. This essay
will review the key aspects of the paper, integrating insights and findings from the
literature cited within the document.
The core contribution of the paper is the design and construction of
DARob, a low-cost and efficient solution for agricultural image data acquisition. The
robot is built with economical components, costing around $8 5 0 , making it a feasible
option for research groups and applications in developing countries. Its modular design
facilitates easy transportation and adaptability to different crop types and sizes.
DARob's capability for automatic operation and remote control enhances the
repeatability and accuracy of data collection, crucial for precision agriculture.

9

Figure 3 View of the DARob with its main systems [33].
The emergence of agricultural robotics, as investigated by [34-35], and
additional researchers, has brought a paradigm shift in agricultural methodologies.
Robots like DARob are tailored for specialized agricultural tasks such as weed
management, crop surveillance, and precision farming, proving to be more effective
than traditional practices. The integration of cutting-edge sensor technologies and
artificial intelligence into these robotic systems has remarkably improved their
performance, a development underscored in the works of [36-37]. Notwithstanding the
substantial advancements in agricultural robotics, the implementation of these
technologies encounters notable challenges, primarily due to their high cost and
complexity. DARob, which is notable for its cost-effective design, seeks to address
these concerns. However, as acknowledged by its creators, DARob confronts specific
limitations such as limited battery autonomy and difficulties in navigating muddy
terrains. These particular challenges are indicative of broader issues prevalent in the
domain of agricultural robotics, as delineated in the research conducted by [38-39],
highlighting the imperative for continued research and development in this field.
The integration of DARob into the realm of precision agriculture marks a
significant progression. The scholarly work delineates DARob's capability in acquiring
high-resolution image data, which proves essential for the segmentation of crops and

10

weeds, an integral aspect of proficient agricultural administration. This functionality is
in concordance with the findings of academics like [40-41]. These scholars underscore
the imperative of acquiring accurate data for efficacious crop management and
elucidate the enhancement of these processes through the application of machine
learning techniques.
2.1.3 Electromagnetic Sensing for Non-Destructive Real-Time Fruit Ripeness
Detection: Case-Study for Automated Strawberry Picking
This study focuses on developing a tool for real-time analysis of strawberry
ripeness using the Thorvald II robotic system, adaptable to various agricultural needs.

3.2 Navigation System Design of the robot
The robot’s navigation system is designed using a high-precision architecture
centered on GNSS-RTK technology to ensure accurate and reliable localization in
outdoor environments. As depicted in Figure 4, the system is structured around four
principal components: the GNSS-RTK base station, the position unit, the processing
unit, and the control unit.
The GNSS-RTK base station, comprising a GNSS antenna and a wireless
transmission module, serves as the source of differential correction data. This correction
data is transmitted to the position unit, which consists of a wireless receiver and a GNSS
module. By combining satellite signals with the received corrections, the position unit
achieves centimeter-level positioning accuracy.
The processing unit, based on an industrial-grade computer, fuses data from the
GNSS and an onboard IMU to enhance localization performance, particularly in areas
with limited satellite visibility. It transmits motion commands to the control unit via the
Controller Area Network (CAN) bus protocol, ensuring low-latency and reliable realtime communication.
The control unit, which includes brushless DC (BLDC) motors, motor controllers,
and encoder sensors, executes the received commands and provides real-time feedback
to support closed-loop control of the robot’s motion.
This integrated system design enables precise, stable, and responsive navigation,
making it highly suitable for applications such as autonomous agricultural machinery

24

and field robotics. Furthermore, the adoption of standardized communication protocols
and modular hardware components enhances the system's reliability, scalability, and
adaptability across diverse operational scenarios [51–52].

3.4 GNSS-RTK and IMU fusion Position Principle
3.4.1 Global Navigation Satellite System Real-Time Kinematic (GNSS RTK)
Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) and Real-Time Kinematic
(RTK) positioning technologies have significantly advanced the field of precision
agriculture, particularly in mobile robotic systems. GNSS-RTK enables centimeterlevel positioning accuracy, which is essential for autonomous operations such as
seeding, fertilizing, and mapping agricultural fields. This level of precision reduces
redundancy, minimizes input waste, and enhances resource efficiency and yield
outcomes. Moreover, GNSS-RTK enhances the reliability of robotic operations under
varying environmental conditions, including changes in weather and lighting, thus
improving overall system productivity.
To apply GNSS data within a Cartesian coordinate system suitable for robotic
navigation, it is necessary to convert geodetic coordinates (latitude and longitude) into
the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate system. The UTM system

28

provides a two-dimensional, metric-based map projection of the Earth’s surface,
simplifying calculations related to distance and direction, which are critical for accurate
motion control[15].
In UTM coordinates, a location is specified using a zone number, hemisphere,
and a pair of easting and northing values. Each zone spans 6° of longitude, with a central
meridian assigned and a false easting of 500,000 meters to prevent negative coordinate
values. For example, an easting of 400,000 meters corresponds to a position roughly
100 kilometers west of the central meridian. In the northern hemisphere, northing
values start at 0 at the equator and increase up to approximately 9.3 million meters at
84°N, whereas in the southern hemisphere, northings begin at 10 million meters and
decrease southward[22].

1984 (WGS 84), which models the Earth as an oblate spheroid with a semi-major axis

3.5 IMU-Based Heading Angle Measurement
The calculation of the rotation or heading of mobile robots is a critical aspect of
autonomous navigation and robotic system design. Accurate determination of a robot’s
orientation is essential for executing tasks such as path planning, obstacle avoidance,
and precise maneuvering within dynamic environments. Inertial Measurement Units
(IMUs) play a fundamental role in this process. These sensors, which typically consist
of gyroscopes and accelerometers, are employed to measure both rotational movements
and linear accelerations of mobile robots. The gyroscope measures the rate of rotation

30

around the robot’s axes, while the accelerometer captures linear acceleration along
these axes. By integrating the data from these two types of sensors, the robot’s
orientation over time can be accurately calculated.

velocity 𝜔(𝑡) measured by the gyroscope. The basic equation for updating the heading

3.6 Trajectory-Tracking Control System
3.6.1 Car-Like Kinematic Model of the Robotic Vehicle
The car-like kinematic model provides a fundamental framework for
representing the motion of four-wheeled, steering-type mobile robots, particularly those
with automobile-like configurations. This model is extensively utilized in autonomous
navigation systems because of its simplicity and its ability to accurately predict motion
trajectories while adhering to nonholonomic constraints [54–55]. It serves as the
foundational basis for many trajectories tracking algorithms, including the widely used
Pure Pursuit method, where the geometric relationships between steering angles,

31

vehicle velocities, and curvature radii are critical for calculating the target point and
generating appropriate control commands.
In the context of Pure Pursuit, the car-like kinematic model enables the robot to
dynamically adjust its steering based on the relative position of a lookahead point along
the planned path. By leveraging the mathematical relationship between the curvature of
the desired path and the required steering angle, the robot is able to continuously update
its heading and maintain smooth and accurate path following. The simplicity and
efficiency of this model make it particularly well-suited for real-time applications in
outdoor environments, where fast response and adaptability to path deviations are
essential.
Furthermore, because the car-like kinematic model captures the nonholonomic
motion constraints inherent to wheeled robots, it provides a realistic and reliable
foundation for designing navigation and control strategies. These characteristics are
especially important in agricultural, field robotics, and other outdoor scenarios, where
unpredictable terrain and environmental disturbances necessitate robust and precise
control mechanisms to ensure stable and effective autonomous operation.
As illustrated in Figure 17, the vehicle's configuration is defined with respect to
the rear axle center (𝑥, 𝑦), its heading angle (𝜃), and the front-wheel steering angle (𝜙).
The wheelbase (𝐿) refers to the distance between the front and rear axles. The forward
velocity is denoted (𝑣1 ), and the steering rate is represented by (𝑣2 ), which collectively

3.6.2 Pure Pursuit algorithm
The Pure Pursuit (PP) algorithm is a geometric-based path-tracking method
that is widely utilized in mobile robotic systems because of its simplicity and
effectiveness. The fundamental principle of the algorithm is to identify a goal point

33

located ahead along the reference trajectory and to compute a circular arc that connects
the vehicle’s current position to this target point. Based on this arc, the algorithm
determines the appropriate front-wheel steering angle required for the vehicle to follow
the desired path. As illustrated in Figure 18, the center of the vehicle’s rear axle, denoted
as (𝑥, 𝑦), is used as the reference point for motion control, while the lookahead point is
represented by ( 𝑥𝑙 , 𝑦𝑙 ). A critical parameter in the Pure Pursuit algorithm is the
lookahead distance 𝐿𝑑 , which defines how far ahead along the path the goal point is
selected. The value of 𝐿𝑑 has a significant influence on the robot's steering behavior
and trajectory smoothness. A shorter lookahead distance results in more aggressive
steering commands, allowing the robot to respond quickly to path deviations but
potentially causing oscillations and instability at higher speeds. Conversely, a longer
lookahead distance generates smoother steering trajectories and improved stability,
although it may reduce the system’s ability to track sharp turns accurately. Therefore,
selecting an appropriate 𝐿𝑑 is crucial for balancing responsiveness and stability, and
in some implementations, 𝐿𝑑 is dynamically adjusted based on the vehicle’s velocity
and operating conditions to optimize path-following performance.

according to the robot’s steering intensity. Reducing speed during sharp turns allows

the robot to maintain better control and reduces the risk of overshooting or deviating
from the planned trajectory. Conversely, maintaining higher speeds during straight-line
motion improves efficiency without compromising stability. These combined strategies
enable the robot to adapt its behavior dynamically to the characteristics of the path,
enhancing overall robustness, motion smoothness, and trajectory-tracking precision.

3.8 Implementation procedure
The overall autonomous navigation system developed in this study integrates
positioning sensors and computational algorithms into a structured architecture, as
illustrated in Figure 22. The system comprises three main components:
• Positioning Sensors: This block includes GNSS RTK modules, IMU
modules, and encoder modules. The GNSS RTK modules provide highaccuracy satellite-based positioning data, while the IMU modules offer
inertial information regarding orientation and velocity. The encoder modules
track wheel rotations or motor positions to support precise motion estimation.
• Computer and Embedded Systems: This block consists of the computational
platform that executes autonomous driving-based algorithms. These
algorithms are categorized into three main types:
• Position Algorithm: Processes raw sensor data to determine the current
position of the robot.
• Localization Algorithm: Integrates data from multiple sensors to ascertain
the robot's location within a predefined coordinate framework or map.
• Navigation Algorithm: Utilizes position and localization outputs to plan,
control, and execute the robot’s movement paths.
• GNSS Base Station: Depicted outside the main autonomous driving block,
the GNSS base station provides differential correction signals to the GNSS

45

RTK modules. This correction mechanism significantly improves the
positioning accuracy required for precise navigation tasks.

Figure 22 Diagram illustrating the components of an autonomous driving system.

The proposed navigation system was implemented following a structured
workflow, as depicted in Figure 23. The procedure begins with system initialization,
including the verification of sensor functionality and communication links. Once
operational integrity is confirmed, localization is performed by fusing data from the
GNSS RTK receiver, IMU, and wheel encoders. A state estimation module integrates
these sensor inputs to generate a reliable pose estimation, which supports subsequent
path planning and path-following tasks.
The system was tested in an open-sky environment under optimal GNSS
conditions, as shown in Figure 24. The test field provided a flat and unobstructed area
measuring approximately 64 × 20 meters, offering ideal conditions for evaluating the
navigation system's baseline performance.
To comprehensively assess the tracking capabilities, three different trajectory

4.2 Performance comparison of path tracking algorithms
To assess the effectiveness of the proposed PP-DSC algorithm, a comparative
evaluation was conducted against the conventional Pure Pursuit (PP) algorithm using
three reference trajectories: straight-line, circular, and figure-eight paths, selected to

50

examine the algorithms' capability in handling directional variability and curvature
transitions.
For the straight-line trajectory (Figure 27(a)), the PP algorithm with a fixed
lookahead distance of 4 meters resulted in an RMSE of 0.22 meters and a maximum
lateral deviation of 0.41 meters, whereas PP-DSC reduced the RMSE to 0.05 meters
and the maximum deviation to 0.09 meters. Oscillations during the initial motion phase
under the PP algorithm, attributed to short lookahead settings, align with prior findings
[58].
Regarding the circular trajectory (Figure 27(b)), PP produced an RMSE of 0.29
meters and a maximum deviation of 0.44 meters. In contrast, PP-DSC, by dynamically
adjusting lookahead distance and velocity before curve entry, achieved an RMSE of
0.07 meters and a maximum deviation of 0.11 meters, thus improving path conformity.
For the figure-eight trajectory (Figure 27(c)), PP exhibited frequent overshoot and
undershoot with an RMSE of 0.24 meters and a maximum deviation of 0.34 meters,
whereas PP-DSC maintained higher stability, achieving an RMSE of 0.08 meters and
capping deviation at 0.13 meters. These results corroborate previous studies [59],
confirming the advantages of dynamic lookahead adjustment under rapidly changing
curvature profiles.
Quantitative performance across all trajectories is presented in Table 7 and
visualized in Figure 28. The results clearly indicate that PP-DSC outperformed the
standard PP algorithm across all error metrics, including mean deviation, RMSE, mean
absolute error (MAE), and maximum deviation. When considered alongside prior
studies [58-59], the findings confirm that dynamically adapting the lookahead distance
and velocity in response to real-time steering conditions plays a critical role in reducing
oscillations, enhancing stability, and achieving high-precision path tracking in diverse
navigation scenarios.

4.3 Speed Adaptation Behavior
The ability to regulate a robot’s velocity based on path geometry is critical for
enhancing tracking accuracy and motion stability in autonomous navigation. This
capability becomes particularly important when the robot encounters directional
changes or varying curvature along the trajectory. The conventional Pure Pursuit (PP)
algorithm employs a fixed lookahead distance and does not adapt the velocity in real
time. In contrast, the proposed Pure Pursuit with Dynamic Lookahead and SteeringBased Speed Control (PP-DSC) algorithm dynamically modulates the robot’s velocity
according to the steering percentage, which is calculated as the ratio between the current
steering angle and the maximum allowable steering angle, as defined in Eq. (30). The
velocity is subsequently adjusted within predefined upper and lower bounds using Eqs.
(31) and (32), enabling the robot to decelerate during sharp turns and accelerate along
straight segments.

In autonomous navigation, the interplay between linear velocity, angular velocity,
and turning radius plays a critical role, as illustrated in Figure 35. The relationship
among these parameters fundamentally determines the maneuverability and stability of
a mobile robot during path tracking. The turning radius 𝑟 can be expressed as the ratio

As shown in the figure, for a given angular velocity, increasing the linear velocity
results in a larger turning radius, meaning the robot follows a gentler curve. Conversely,
for a fixed linear velocity, increasing the angular velocity leads to a smaller turning
radius, enabling the robot to execute sharper turns.
This interaction highlights the need for coordinated control of both linear and angular
velocities to maintain desired path curvature, especially when adapting to varying path
geometries. In straight-line motion, where 𝜔 approaches zero, the turning radius tends
toward infinity, allowing the robot to maintain high speeds. In contrast, when
navigating tight curves requiring high angular velocities, the linear velocity must often
be reduced to achieve a smaller, controllable turning radius without sacrificing pathfollowing accuracy or causing instability.

Understanding and managing this dynamic relationship is essential for designing
navigation algorithms such as Pure Pursuit with Dynamic Lookahead and SteeringBased Speed Control (PP-DSC), which leverage adjustments in velocity profiles based

CONCLUSION

This study presented the development of an advanced navigation framework for
steering-type autonomous mobile robots, introducing an enhanced version of the Pure
Pursuit (PP) algorithm known as Pure Pursuit with Dynamic Steering Control (PPDSC). The system was implemented on a ROS 2 platform and integrated multiple
sensors including GNSS-RTK, IMU, and wheel encoders to achieve high-precision
localization and robust trajectory tracking under various path conditions.
The primary conclusions drawn from the study's findings reveal several key
characteristics associated with autonomous navigation performance:
• The PP-DSC algorithm dynamically adjusts the lookahead distance and
linear velocity based on the steering demand, resulting in improved path-tracking
accuracy compared to the conventional PP algorithm.
• The enhanced algorithm reduced the mean lateral deviation to 0.05 meters
on straight paths and maintained deviations below 0.08 meters on curved paths,
outperforming the maximum deviation of 0.44 meters observed with standard PP.
• The dynamic adjustment strategy effectively stabilized the robot’s motion
during complex trajectories such as loops and figure-eight patterns.
• The system achieved reliable performance at speeds up to 5.0 meters per
second, demonstrating real-time localization robustness even under varied field
conditions.
These results emphasize the importance of adaptive control strategies in improving
both motion stability and trajectory tracking precision. The success of the PP-DSC
algorithm confirms that real-time adjustments of control parameters in response to the
robot’s dynamics are essential for dependable autonomous navigation, particularly in
outdoor agricultural applications.
This research proposes a practical and effective approach for improving the
performance of autonomous navigation systems, especially in contexts where high
positioning accuracy and real-time responsiveness are critical. While this study was

64

conducted using a mobile robot prototype, the proposed framework is highly adaptable
and can be applied to various types of agricultural machinery such as autonomous
tractors, tillers, and field robots. This can improve operational efficiency, reduce labor
requirements, and support the advancement of precision agriculture.
Moreover, the developed PP-DSC algorithm holds strong potential for application
in agricultural robotics. Its ability to dynamically adjust the lookahead distance and
linear velocity allows autonomous systems to maintain precise trajectories even in
narrow crop rows or structured field environments. This makes it highly suitable for
use in tasks such as autonomous spraying, seeding, harvesting, or row-following
operations in orchards and vineyards, where accuracy and consistency are essential to
avoid crop damage and improve operational efficiency. The algorithm’s modular
design also allows it to be integrated into a wide range of mobile platforms used in
precision agriculture.
Future development of the system may focus on expanding the algorithm’s
capabilities to support obstacle avoidance, refining parameter tuning for high-speed
operations, and incorporating machine learning techniques to improve adaptability in
dynamic and unpredictable environments. These enhancements would contribute to the
continued advancement of autonomous systems in both agricultural and industrial
applications. Although the current framework has demonstrated reliable performance
in flat, planar environments, it has not yet been tested in terrains with elevation changes
or irregular surfaces such as slopes, hills, or rugged agricultural fields. In such
conditions, additional factors including gravitational effects, slip dynamics, and tiltinduced localization errors may significantly influence the control performance and
stability of the robot. Nevertheless, the system possesses strong potential for further
development toward slope-capable navigation. With the integration of terrain
inclination estimation, slope-aware path planning, and tilt-compensated control
mechanisms, the framework could be extended to achieve precise and stable navigation
across a wide range of real-world topographies.
In conclusion, the research successfully achieves its original objectives by
delivering an intelligent control system that enables accurate positioning and responsive
maneuvering, along with a robust navigation framework that supports real-time
autonomous operation in dynamic and wide-open environments.
